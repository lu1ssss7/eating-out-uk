Britain is eating out again, but not like it did before Covid

Five years after lockdowns emptied the nation's restaurants and pubs, official data shows the share of food spending made outside the home has still not returned to its pre-pandemic level

By Luis Eyherachar, data reporter, 27 November 2026

For almost two decades, the way Britons split their food budget barely moved. Roughly one pound in every four spent on food and non-alcoholic drinks went on meals, snacks and coffees bought outside the home; the rest went into the shopping basket. Then the pandemic broke that pattern in a single year, and the latest government figures suggest it has not fully mended.

According to the Family Food survey, published by the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra), the share of food spending made outside the home stood at 29.0% in 2019-20. In 2020-21, the year of lockdowns, it collapsed to 7.9%. By 2024-25 it had climbed back to 22.7%: a clear recovery, but still 6.3 percentage points below where it was before Covid.

The three key figures shown with the chart below summarise that story. Select any of them to find the corresponding moment on the chart.

Two decades of stability

Figure 1 shows the share of food spending eaten out from 2001-02 to 2024-25. For most of that period the line is strikingly flat, hovering between about 26% and 29%. There is a small dip after the 2008 financial crisis (the share fell to 25.7% in 2009), followed by a slow climb to its 2019-20 peak.

That stability matters. It tells us that the shock of 2020 was not a continuation of an existing trend, but a break with one. You can also click any point to see the figures for that year, or use the “At home” and “Both” buttons to see the mirror image: in 2020-21, 92.1% of food spending went on food bought for the home.

The year the restaurants closed

The fall in 2020-21 is the steepest movement in the entire series. It reflects the forced closure of the hospitality sector during successive lockdowns rather than a sudden change in taste. Households did not stop eating; they moved their meals, and their money, back into their own kitchens.

That year also changed how the data was collected. The survey, which asks households to record what they buy over two weeks, switched from face-to-face to telephone interviews. The 2020-21 figure should be read as the best available estimate of an extraordinary year rather than a precise measurement.

A recovery that stalled

Restrictions lifted, and eating out came back quickly: the share rose to 19.4% in 2021-22. Since then, however, the recovery has been slow, adding only around a point a year. If the share had simply returned to its pre-pandemic level, almost three in every ten pounds spent on food would now go on eating out. Instead it is closer to two in ten.

The timing points to a second pressure. The post-Covid years coincided with the sharpest rise in food prices in a generation: the Office for National Statistics reported in April 2023 that food and non-alcoholic drink prices had risen by 19.2% over the previous 12 months, the fastest rate in more than 45 years. When budgets are squeezed, a meal out is often among the first things to go.

The data cannot prove that the cost-of-living crisis is holding eating out back. But it does show that the recovery slowed just as household budgets came under the greatest strain.

Does alcohol change the picture?

Alcohol is the item most under-reported in this kind of survey, so Figure 1 is based on food and non-alcoholic drinks only. Tick “Include alcohol” to add the same measure with drinks included. The line sits a few points higher, because more of what Britons spend on alcohol goes on pubs and bars, but the shape is identical: a plateau, a collapse and an incomplete recovery. Excluding alcohol, Britain has made back about 70% of the ground it lost in 2020-21; including it, about 73%.

What the numbers cannot tell us

These are national averages per person. They do not show which households have returned to restaurants and which have not, and they cannot tell us whether the gap reflects choice or necessity. Defra publishes the same survey broken down by income, region and household type, and those figures will be essential to understanding who is missing from Britain's restaurants.

The shares in Figure 1 are calculated from spending in current prices. Because both sides of the comparison are measured in the same pounds, a general rise in prices does not distort the share in itself; but if restaurant prices rose faster than supermarket prices, part of the change could reflect costs rather than habits.

What is clear is that eating out, a central part of British social life before 2020, has not reclaimed its place. Whether that gap closes may depend less on public health than on the state of household finances.

About the data

Source: Defra, Family Food datasets (UKExp_17Sep26, published 17 September 2026), based on the Living Costs and Food Survey. The share eaten out is calculated as spending on food and non-alcoholic drinks for consumption outside the home divided by total spending on food and non-alcoholic drinks. Periods from 2006 to 2014 are calendar years; other periods are financial years (April to March). Inflation figure: Office for National Statistics, Consumer price inflation, UK: March 2023, published 19 April 2023.
